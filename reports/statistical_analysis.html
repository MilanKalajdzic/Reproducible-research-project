Statistical Diagnostics and Time-Series Baseline

Overview

This report presents the statistical diagnostics and time-series baseline analysis for the ETF prediction project. The analysis focuses on four ETFs: IEUR, FEZ, EUFN, and IVV. For each ETF, we evaluated feature variance, pairwise correlation redundancy, Random Forest feature importance, and a univariate ARIMA baseline on the closing-price series.

Feature Variance Analysis

The processed datasets contain a large number of technical indicators, with approximately 263 to 266 features per ETF. The highest-variance indicators differ slightly across ETFs, but several variables appear repeatedly near the top, including INC_1, DEC_1, AOBV_LR_2, AOBV_SR_2, and AMATe_*. This suggests that a subset of trend- and momentum-related indicators exhibits greater dispersion over time.

Variance should not be interpreted as predictive importance on its own. In this project, it is used as an initial descriptive diagnostic to identify features that vary meaningfully across observations.

Correlation Redundancy

A major finding of the analysis is the very high redundancy in the feature space. For each ETF, we counted the number of feature pairs with absolute correlation above 0.95. The totals are very large, indicating that many technical indicators contain nearly identical information.

Feature Importance

To assess predictive usefulness, we fitted a Random Forest classifier for each ETF and extracted feature-importance scores. The most important indicators are relatively consistent across ETFs, especially the SMC* family, along with indicators such as CRSI_3_2_100, BBP_5_2.0_2.0, THERMO_20_2_0.5, CFO_9, BOP, and short-horizon return features.

The ADF p-values are high for all four ETFs, which indicates that the raw closing-price series are non-stationary. This supports the use of differencing, and therefore the ARIMA(1,1,1) specification is a sensible baseline.

The ARIMA results are not directly comparable with the Random Forest classification results, because ARIMA forecasts a continuous price series, while the Random Forest predicts the binary target \(\Gamma\). For that reason, ARIMA should be interpreted here as a univariate forecasting baseline rather than a direct competitor to the classification model.

Interpretation

The analysis produces three main conclusions:

  1. The technical-indicator feature space is large and highly redundant.
  2. Despite this redundancy, a relatively small subset of indicators appears consistently important across ETFs.
  3. The ETF closing-price series are non-stationary, so classical time-series baselines require differencing and should be interpreted separately from directional classification models.

These findings support the broader modeling strategy of combining technical-indicator engineering with predictive modeling, while also emphasizing the importance of reproducibility, feature selection, and careful interpretation.

Limitations

One limitation is that the Random Forest and ARIMA models address different prediction tasks and use different evaluation metrics. Another important limitation is that preprocessing and scaling decisions in the broader pipeline should be checked carefully to avoid leakage in the final modeling workflow.
